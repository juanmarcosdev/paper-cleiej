\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Social networks have a strong impact on \emph{opinion formation}, often
resulting in polarization. Individuals express their views, are exposed to
the views of others, and may revise their own beliefs in response. Whether
this feedback loop ends in broad consensus or in entrenched polarization
depends on the structure of the network and on the behavioral rules that
govern each interaction. Formal models of this process allow us to
characterize precisely the conditions under which each outcome arises.

The DeGroot model~\cite{DeGroot1974} is one of the most prominent formalisms
for social learning and opinion formation~\cite{Castellano2009}. A community is
represented as a weighted directed graph, the \emph{influence graph}, whose
nodes are \emph{agents} and whose edges encode how strongly agents influence
one another. Each agent holds an opinion in $[0,1]$, which indicates the
strength of its agreement with a given proposition. Agents repeatedly revise
their opinions by taking a weighted average of the opinions of those who
influence them. A fundamental theoretical result guarantees convergence to
\emph{consensus} whenever the influence graph is strongly connected and agents
retain non-zero self-influence~\cite{DeGroot1974,Castellano2009}. The
significance of this result lies in the fact that consensus is a central
problem in social learning. Indeed, the inability to reach consensus is a sign
of a polarized community.

Nevertheless, the DeGroot model makes two assumptions that can be overly
constraining in real social networks. First, it assumes that agents
\emph{always} express their opinions, disregarding the social pressure that
may lead individuals to withhold their views. Second, it assumes that agents
\emph{linearly} assimilate the opinions of those who influence them, ignoring
the nonlinear psychological responses that shape how people react to
disagreement. Two well-documented phenomena challenge these assumptions: the
\emph{Spiral of Silence} and \emph{cognitive biases}, respectively.

The Spiral of Silence, introduced by
Noelle-Neumann~\cite{NoelleNeumann1974,NoelleNeumann1984}, posits that
individuals who perceive their opinion to be in the minority tend to
\emph{self-censor}. They withhold expression to avoid social isolation, and
their silence reinforces the dominance of the majority view. Empirical studies
confirm that this mechanism is active both offline and
online~\cite{Glynn1997,Hampton2014,Hampton2019,Neubaum2018,Sohn2022}.
Simulation studies show that it can substantially distort the collective
opinion landscape in digitally mediated
networks~\cite{Sohn2022,Wang2013,Ross2019,Cabrera2021}. Aranda et
al.~\cite{aranda2024sound} recently gave a formal treatment of this mechanism:
they extended the DeGroot model with silence and derived conditions under
which consensus forms or fails. That work is the direct predecessor of the
present paper.

Cognitive biases add a further source of nonlinearity. Individuals do not
respond neutrally to information that conflicts with their beliefs.
\emph{Confirmation bias} leads them to discount disagreeing views; the
\emph{backfire effect} makes them harden their positions when confronted with
contrary evidence; \emph{authority bias} amplifies the influence of perceived
high-status sources; and \emph{insular bias} makes them ignore external
opinions altogether~\cite{Aronson2010,Nyhan2010,Friedrich2020,Alvim2024}.
These distortions change how agents assimilate and reject information, and
they can foster polarization in ways that the DeGroot model cannot capture.
Alvim et al.~\cite{Alvim2024} encoded these four biases as nonlinear
\emph{bias functions} applied to opinion disagreements, and derived
conditions under which consensus is preserved or fails in the synchronous
DeGroot model. Their framework does not consider silence. Conversely, the
model of Aranda et al.~\cite{aranda2024sound} considers silence but not
cognitive biases.

In this paper, we combine both phenomena in two \emph{Cognitive-Bias Silence
Opinion Models} (CBSOM). The state of the system records, for each agent, an
opinion in $[0,1]$ and whether the agent is \emph{vocal} or \emph{silent}. At
each time unit, every agent performs two actions. First, it revises its
opinion: the disagreement with each influencer passes through a bias function,
which determines how much of that disagreement the agent actually absorbs.
Second, it decides whether to speak in the next round: it remains vocal only
if enough of its influencers hold opinions close to its own, within a
\emph{tolerance radius}. Silent agents still revise their opinions; they only
stop expressing them. The two models differ in what silent agents contribute.
In the memoryless model \CBSOMminus, silent agents exert no influence. In the
memory-based model \CBSOMplus, they keep influencing others through the last
opinion that they expressed.

We focus on the problem of convergence to consensus. We first show that the
bias layer does not interfere with two structural properties of the
memoryless base model \SOMminus~\cite{aranda2024sound}: global silence never
lasts more than one round, and the maximum and minimum opinions contract
monotonically. Both properties hold for every continuous bias in the
\emph{receptive-resistant} region of~\cite{Alvim2024}, i.e., for biases that
move an agent towards an influencer without overshooting. We then restrict
our attention to the \emph{scaled linear} biases $f_\alpha(x)=\alpha x$ with
$\alpha\in(0,1)$, and prove consensus on cliques of every size $n\ge 2$. The
case $n=2$ is of particular interest: on two-agent cliques, \SOMminus\ can
oscillate forever between two opinions, and every scaled linear bias removes
this oscillation. Finally, we use large-scale simulations, with up to $10^{6}$
agents, to study the settings that our formal analysis does not yet cover:
non-clique graphs, biases outside the receptive-resistant region, mixtures of
biases, and memory.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that embeds cognitive
bias functions into a Spiral-of-Silence model and gives formal consensus
guarantees for the resulting models.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} The contributions of this paper are the
following:

\begin{enumerate}

  \item We introduce \CBSOMminus\ and \CBSOMplus, two Cognitive-Bias Silence
    Opinion Models that couple confirmation, backfire, authority, and insular
    bias functions with memoryless and memory-based update rules,
    respectively (Section~\ref{sec:model}).

  \item We prove that two structural properties of the base model
    \SOMminus~\cite{aranda2024sound}, namely the impossibility of perpetual
    global silence and the monotonic contraction of extreme opinions, are
    preserved under every continuous bias function in the
    receptive-resistant region (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

  \item For the scaled linear biases
    $\mathcal{F}_\alpha = \{f_\alpha(x)=\alpha x \mid \alpha\in(0,1)\}$,
    we prove consensus in cliques for all $n\ge 2$. This removes the period-2
    oscillation that prevents consensus on two-agent cliques in the base
    model (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

  \item Through simulations on uniform and non-uniform networks with up to
    $10^{6}$ agents, we show that (i)~confirmation bias, which lies in the
    receptive-resistant region, largely preserves consensus; (ii)~backfire and
    authority biases prevent consensus even in dense graphs; (iii)~the density
    needed for consensus grows only sub-linearly with network size; and
    (iv)~memory suppresses consensus almost entirely as networks grow
    (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\end{enumerate}
